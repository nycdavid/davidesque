Consoles definitely are not a trivial cost, especially for those of us who are budget-conscious and try to 
cut expenses where we can. The particularly tough part about gaming as a hobby is that they get you on both 
ends. You have to drop money on the hardware but there's an ongoing "maintenance cost" of buying games that
you want to play.

What if you could both remove that ongoing cost and eventually have the console pay for itself?

That's exactly what I've done by leveraging my __local library__ to borrow the video games that I'm interested
in playing. It costs nothing to borrow and the monetary value of the games becomes a net positive for me that
eventually makes the cost of the console a wash. Borrow enough games, and the purchase of the console has 
effectively unlocked something of a value creation engine.

# Calculating break-even: make a balance sheet

Like anything, it's good to go into this with a plan, even if it's simple.

I recommend creating a simple balance spreadsheet that records the amount of money spent on hardware vs. the 
amount gained by borrowing games. Your balance will start negative because you'll initially be down the cost
of the console but over time, as you play more games, you'll increasingly reap more value. Eventually you'll 
get to 0 and beyond, at which point your purchase has actually begun to _earn money_.

# How to get the (accurate) price of games

It's very important that we, as meticulous, money-minded people, not lie to ourselves about the true
cost of a particular financial decision. In this case, we must be precise about how we calculate just
how much a particular game defrays the original cost of the game console.

For instance, some of the prices above might seem a touch _low_. Take __Final Fantasy XVI__, for instance. I've priced it
at $25, which is _half_ the price it costs on the [PlayStation store](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/final-fantasy-xvi/).
Similarly, nearly all the games on this list are priced quite a bit below their "Brand New" price.

This is accurate for me because I know myself: no matter how badly I wanted to play a game, I would _never pay full 
price for it_. For FF XVI, when I was looking up the price, I clearly saw that it was on sale on Steam for 50% off, so
that's the price I used. Even if it hadn't been on sale, I would have done my due diligence and looked at 
historical pricing trends for it and, in all likelihood, would have waited for it to be on sale.

Similarly, for The Last of Us series, I have part 2 on the sheet but not part 1, even though I do plan on borrowing
part 1 and playing it on the PS5. Why is that? It's because I __already own__ part 1 on PS3. I was able to play it for
free at that point in time already, thus it does nothing to offset the cost of the PS5.

_That's_ the important question to ask yourself when pricing games for the balance sheet: __what would I pay to 
play this game today?__

Pay special attention to how you calculate your pricing, because it's the basis for this whole technique.

# It may not be for everyone (but how you can make it for you)

This strategy isn't without its drawbacks. You're working within the constraints of the library system and that comes
with real limitations:

- __Availability__: your local library just might not have the game that you want and has
  no plans to stock it. That's why doing the planning step above _before making the purchase_ is so crucial.
- __Borrowing windows__: your library may only lend for a week at a time and not allow any renewals. Is that 
  enough time for you? Is the annoyance of returning a game prematurely and then having to borrow it again a dealbreaker?
- __Fines and penalties__: returning any item late to the library will most likely result in fines. Losing or damaging
  a game could result in __heavy__ fines. These just aren't things you have to deal with when you own the game outright.
- __Waitlists__: is the game popular? Be prepared to wait. Libraries only have a specific number of copies
  in circulation. If they're all checked out, people get added to the waitlist and that waitlist could be 30-40 people 
  long. It could be _months_ before your turn comes up. 

All of the above can be solved with proper planning and flexibility, though:

1. Most libraries have online, searchable databases of their catalog. Make sure to do your research and ensure that
   the games you want to play are available in the catalog and that there are enough of them to justify your purchase.
2. Know the parameters of how your library loans out items like how long they lend out for and how many renewals are
   available to you. Make sure to tailor your borrowing to those terms and what you've got going on in your life. Super
   busy over the next 2 weeks with almost no time to play? Don't borrow that 50 hour RPG that you have to return in 10 
   days.
3. Make sure you're able to adhere to deadlines. If your library doesn't have an automated reminder system, like an 
   email before the day an item is due, set reminders and calendar events for return dates and stick to them. Also,
   make sure the library is convenient to get to and not very out of the way for you. Eliminate any reasons not to 
   return things on time.
4. When dealing with a popular game and waitlists, make sure to enter the queue as soon as you can and use that time
   to work through your backlog of other games. 

# Borrow vs. Buy

There are definitely some instances where it may be worth considering purchasing a game outright. One of the instances 
would be when a game has inherent replay value that'll be hard to take advantage of when you can only borrow the game 
for a specific amount of time. Or maybe there's a game that you want to play online with your friends and it's on an 
ongoing basis.

In these cases, I think purchasing the game would make sense. See if you can get it secondhand on Facebook Marketplace
or Craigslist, but don't feel like you can't buy it. If it's a good that you can extract value from on a repeated 
basis, it could very well pay for itself over time in personal enjoyment.

# Pure profit

After your break-even point, every subsequent game you borrow and play is _pure profit_. Meaning, the PS5 you bought
is now actually a net-positive asset. Even if you stored it in the closet and never played it again, you'd be overall
better off than before you bought it. 

Furthermore, the fact that more games get released as time goes on means that you have the opportunity to unlock
more and more value from the console by continuing to borrow and play games for free.

What if you've worked through the list of games on your break-even list and decided that this framework really isn't 
for you or that you don't have as much time to game as you thought you would? If you've taken good care of the console, 
you could even _resell_ it on the used market and still be net positive.
